TabPFN v2

Setup · Inference · Training · Prior · KV caching · Time series

Part two: how TabPFN v2 works at inference, how it is trained, and what makes it fast.

Setup

PRETRAINED ONCE

Tabular foundation model

θ fixed, no gradient steps on D
ŷ ~ q_θ(y | x, D_train)
one forward pass

PRETRAINING TARGET

q_θ(y | x, D) ≈ ∫ p(y | x, φ) p(φ | D) dφ

φ: a data-generating mechanism drawn from a prior (for TabPFN: a structural causal model)

Standard workflow: fit a model per dataset, tune hyperparameters, predict. Tabular foundation models are trained once. At prediction time the training set is passed as input together with the test rows, and the model outputs predictions in one forward pass. There is no gradient step on the user's data. The pretraining objective makes the model approximate the posterior predictive distribution under a prior over data-generating mechanisms. In TabPFN that prior is a distribution over structural causal models.

How TabPFN v2 is used. The input is the whole table: training rows with their labels and test rows without. The model runs one forward pass over all of it and outputs a predictive distribution for each test row: class probabilities for classification, a density over bins for regression. The weights never change. In the scikit-learn style API, fit only stores and preprocesses the data; the real work happens in predict. The next slides explain how pretraining makes this work.

TabPFN v2: training loop

1

Sample a causal graph and its functions φ

2

Sample a table D from φ

3

Split: ≤2,048 train rows, 128 test rows

4

Predict test labels from train rows, one pass

5

Cross-entropy on test labels, update θ

repeat: ~2M steps × 64 tables per batch ≈ 130M synthetic tables

Only synthetic tables during pretraining. No real dataset.

One training step. Sample a structural causal model from the prior. Sample a table from it. Split the rows into a training part, up to 2,048 rows, and 128 held-out rows. Feed everything to the transformer; it predicts the held-out labels conditioned on the training rows. The loss is cross-entropy on those held-out labels. Repeat about two million steps with 64 tables per batch, so roughly 130 million synthetic tables. Because the task changes every step, the network learns a general procedure for predicting from examples rather than one dataset's function.

How one synthetic table is generated. Sample a directed acyclic graph with a scale-free generator. Each edge applies a random function: a small MLP with one of several activations, a decision-tree-like rule, or a discretization, plus Gaussian noise. Pick some nodes as features and one as the target; the rest stay hidden. Features can sit upstream or downstream of the target. Categorical features come from mapping a vector to its nearest random prototype. Post-processing adds Kumaraswamy warping, quantization and missing values. Sizes during pretraining: up to 2,048 training rows, 1 to 160 features, up to 10 classes, under 75 thousand cells.

TabPFN v2: one draw from the prior

A simplified version of the prior, running live. Every few seconds it samples a new causal graph, random functions on the edges, and 260 rows. One node becomes the target and is binned into classes; two other nodes are shown as features. Features can be upstream or downstream of the target, or unrelated. The real prior is larger: up to 160 features, more function types, warping, quantization and missing values. Pretraining sees about 130 million of these.

Training numbers. About 2 million steps with 64 tables each, so roughly 130 million tables. One node with eight RTX 2080 Ti GPUs for about two weeks. The default model has 12 layers, embedding size 192, 6 heads. The paper does not state a parameter count; about 7 million is my calculation from the released config. Loss is cross-entropy on held-out rows. Classification and regression are separate checkpoints. For regression the output is a distribution over 5,000 bins whose edges are quantiles of targets sampled from the prior, trained with cross-entropy over bins. This gives a full predictive distribution, not only a point estimate.

At inference, TabPFN v2 runs a small ensemble. Each member sees the same data with a different feature order, a different permutation of class labels, and a different preprocessing choice, for example quantile transform, power transform, added SVD features or one-hot encoding. Four members for classification, eight for regression. Outputs are averaged with softmax temperature 0.9. Limits: 10 thousand rows, 500 features, 10 classes. Memory grows with the number of cells, under a kilobyte each. Headline result from the paper: in 2.8 seconds, default TabPFN beat an ensemble of baselines tuned for four hours.

Why TabPFN v2 can cache. In column attention, training rows attend only to training rows; test rows attend to training rows but nobody attends to test rows. So the training rows' states at every layer depend only on the training data. Fit runs the training rows through all 12 layers once and stores each layer's keys and values. Predict then runs only the test rows: at each layer they do their own row attention and read the cached keys and values for column attention. Per layer, the cost drops from recomputing everything, (n plus t) times d squared plus d times (n plus t) squared, to t times d squared plus t times n times d. The paper reports about 300 times faster prediction at 10 thousand rows and 10 features, and about 800 times at 100 features, on CPU. The trade-off is memory. A rough estimate, using 12 layers, 192-wide keys and values, features grouped in pairs and 16-bit floats: about 55 megabytes for a thousand rows and 10 features, 550 megabytes for ten thousand rows and 10 features, 4.7 gigabytes at 100 features, and about 23 gigabytes at the 500-feature limit, per ensemble member. These are my estimates from the model configuration, not published numbers. TabPFN-3 reduces this by sharing one key-value head across test queries, an eight-fold smaller cache. On pricing: Prior Labs meters the TabPFN-3.5 API in tokens. Each call's token count grows with the number of training and test rows, the number of columns and the number of estimators, with a minimum of 10,000 tokens per operation. The Free plan includes TabPFN-3.5-Plus with Thinking mode and has default limits of 5 million tokens per day and 20 million per month. Pro and Max plans are priced by quote; Pro adds an extended KV cache. Since TabPFN-3.5, predictions that successfully reuse the KV cache are charged 75 percent less, which is this slide's idea showing up in the bill. There is no public price per token. On AWS Marketplace the TabPFN-3.5-Plus software is free for non-commercial use and you pay only for the instance hours.

TabPFN-TS shows how far the tabular model generalizes. It uses the unchanged TabPFN v2 regression checkpoint, with no time-series pretraining and no finetuning. Each time step becomes a row. The features are a running index, calendar features for eight cycles from second-of-minute to month-of-year encoded as sine and cosine, plus the year, and seasonal features: the five strongest periods found by an FFT of the detrended series, again as sine and cosine. Known future covariates such as holidays are added as extra columns. There are no lag features. The history rows are the training set, the future rows are the test set, and all future steps are predicted in one forward pass, with quantiles read from TabPFN's distribution over bins. On fev-bench's 28 covariate tasks it is the best model overall, ahead of TiRex, Chronos-Bolt, Toto, Moirai-2 and Sundial. On GIFT-Eval's 97 univariate tasks it ranks just behind TiRex, Toto and Moirai-2, on par with TimesFM 2.0 and Chronos-Bolt. Limits: it cannot extrapolate a trend beyond the range of values it has seen, and it is about 30 times slower than Chronos-Bolt.

Real data helped v2. Better synthetic priors helped more.

Sources: Garg et al., arXiv 2507.03971; arXiv 2605.13986, 2602.11139; HF blog (Kumo). *Self-reported.

Real-TabPFN tested whether real data helps. It takes TabPFN v2, which is pretrained only on synthetic data, and continues pretraining on a small, curated set of large real-world tables. That beat the default TabPFN v2 on most of 29 datasets from the OpenML AutoML Benchmark, and a curated set worked better than broad corpora like CommonCrawl or GitTables. Prior Labs also released Real-TabPFN-2.5, finetuned on 43 real datasets. Since then, models trained only on synthetic data have passed it. TabPFN-3 reports 72 Elo over a tuned and ensembled Real-TabPFN-2.5 and says it was pretrained exclusively on synthetic data. TabICLv2 beats the tuned and ensembled Real-TabPFN-2.5 without tuning. NVIDIA's Kumo Tabular, trained only on synthetic tables, reports the top TabArena score. TabPFN-3.5's report describes the same synthetic pipeline and does not mention real data; its paid Plus and Thinking variants are not documented. So real data was a useful patch for v2, but the bigger gains came from better synthetic priors and architectures.

TabICL, v2 · TabPFN-3, 3.5 · TabFM · Kumo Tabular

The later models fall into two designs. Design A is TabPFN v2's: every cell keeps its own vector through all layers, with attention along rows and columns. Cost per layer grows with n times d squared plus d times n squared. TabPFN-2.5, Mitra and LimiX stay with this design. Design B was introduced by TabICL. Column and row attention run for a few layers, then each row is compressed into one vector: learned CLS tokens are appended to each row, attend over its cells, and their outputs become the row vector. The blue cell at the end of each row is the CLS token, and the blue bars are its outputs. These costs are for a forward pass at inference. A deep in-context learning transformer then works on n row vectors instead of n times d cells. This is what makes million-row inputs feasible. TabPFN itself switched to design B in version 3, and Google's TabFM and NVIDIA's Kumo Tabular are both design B.

How Prior Labs' own line moved away from v2. TabPFN-2.5 keeps the per-cell design but doubles the depth to 24 layers for classification, embeds features in groups of three, and adds 64 learned rows that act as extra compute and attention sinks. A variant is finetuned on 43 real datasets. TabPFN-3 switches to design B, with the column, row and ICL stages from TabICL and the same log-length attention scaling. Test rows share one key-value head, which cuts the cache eight-fold, and rows are processed in chunks; together these get to a million rows. Classes up to 160 via a retrieval-style decoder. TabPFN-3.5 is about four times larger, adds Fourier and empirical-CDF features per cell, and trains one model for both classification and regression. The thinking mode spends more compute at inference; Prior Labs has not published how it works.

TABICL · 2025

Introduced compress-then-ICL. Trained up to 60K rows.

TABICLV2 · 2026

Long-context attention scaling, new prior. 1M rows in <50 GB GPU; fully open.

Sources: Qu et al., arXiv 2502.05564 (TabICL); arXiv 2602.11139 (TabICLv2)

TabICL is the origin of design B. Stage one treats each column as a set and embeds every cell with a Set Transformer using induced attention, which is linear in the number of rows. Stage two runs three layers of attention across the features of each row, with rotary position encoding, and reads out four CLS tokens, concatenated into a 512-dimensional row vector. Stage three is a 12-layer transformer over row vectors, where test rows attend to training rows. TabICL was trained on a mix of SCM and tree-based priors with a curriculum up to 60 thousand rows. TabICLv2 changes the attention scaling: queries are scaled by a learned function of the log context length, which helps generalize to longer contexts than seen in training. It also uses a new prior based on random Cauchy graphs, the Muon optimizer, and quantile regression with 999 quantiles. It handles a million rows by 500 features in under 50 GB of GPU memory.

LimiX from StableAI and Tsinghua is the clearest example of a tabular foundation model doing more than classification and regression. Instead of learning only p of y given x, it learns the joint distribution of all cells given the context, by masking cells during pretraining and reconstructing them. At inference the mask defines the task. Mask the label of the query rows and it is a classifier or regressor. Mask missing feature cells and it imputes. The first LimiX paper also uses it to generate data. LimiX-2 adds causal structure: make each variable the target in turn and look at which features its attention concentrates on; thresholding those scores gives an undirected causal skeleton. Across six standard causal datasets this reaches a mean F1 of 0.797, against 0.659 for the next best model, EXAONE Tabular. It still scores 1935 Elo on TabArena by its own report. Imputation and anomaly scoring are described but not benchmarked in the LimiX-2 paper.

Mitra from Amazon keeps TabPFN v2's per-cell architecture and changes only what it is trained on. Half of its pretraining tables come from structural causal models, the other half from randomly fitted tree models such as gradient boosting, random forests, decision trees and extra trees. The idea is to teach the model the kind of axis-aligned, piecewise structure that trees capture well. It was pretrained on about 37 million tables with at most 16 features, is about 72 million parameters, and is Apache-licensed and finetunable through AutoGluon. Compared with TabICL, which already used 30 percent tree-based SCMs, Mitra goes further on tree priors but stays with the per-cell design.

Google's TabFM follows the compress-rows design that TabICL introduced, but at much larger scale. Cells are embedded in groups of three features with learned Fourier features, where TabPFN v2 embeds pairs linearly. Column and row attention compress each row into eight CLS tokens, a 1024-dimensional vector, twice TabICL's 512. A 24-layer, 1024-wide in-context learning transformer then works over rows. Of 409 million parameters, 403 million are in that ICL stage. It trains on SCM tables only, with contexts up to 16 thousand rows, eight times TabPFN v2's 2 thousand. It is served in BigQuery; the weights are non-commercial.

TabDPT from Layer6, TD Bank's lab, is the main model trained on real data rather than a synthetic prior. It uses 123 OpenML datasets, about 32 million rows, and makes a self-supervised task by picking a random column as the target. Each row is a single token, so it does not keep per-cell vectors. Instead of using all training rows as context, it retrieves the k nearest rows to each query, both in training and at inference, which lets it handle large tables. It is 78 million parameters with 16 layers, limited to 100 features and 10 classes, and reaches accuracy on par with TabPFN v2. Unlike Real-TabPFN, which only continued v2's training on real data, TabDPT is real-data trained from the start.

KumoRFM-2 is Kumo's relational model, released in April before the NVIDIA deal. The input is a whole database. Each row is a node and foreign keys are edges. For a prediction about one entity at time t, it samples a two-hop subgraph around that entity using only data from before t. The task is written in a query language, for example: will this user place zero orders in the next 30 days. In-context examples come from the same database: replay it at earlier anchor times and compute what happened in the following window, up to about ten thousand examples. Inside, it runs column and row attention within each table, then attention across foreign keys and across the context examples, with the context labels injected early. The chart shows why the relational part matters. On RelBench classification, TabPFN-2.5 given only the entity table reaches 61.6 AUROC; flattening the database into one table for TabPFN reaches 76.0; the best supervised relational model reaches 78.1; KumoRFM-2 reaches 79.6 without task-specific training. These numbers are from Kumo's own paper.

This slide collects what each paper reports about training compute, converted to hours on one node of eight H100 GPUs, plus each model's documented size limits. Only a few papers report compute. TabPFN v2 trained for about two weeks on eight RTX 2080 Ti GPUs, roughly 2,700 GPU hours; assuming an H100 is about ten times faster, that is around 35 node-hours. TabICL took 60 A100-days, about 90 node-hours. TabICLv2 took 24.5 H100-days, about 74 node-hours. Mitra took about 60 hours on eight A100s, about 30 node-hours. Real-TabPFN added only a small continued-pretraining run on one GPU. TabPFN-3 does not report compute, but it reports more than 8 trillion training tokens with 53 million parameters; the standard estimate of six times parameters times tokens, at about 40 percent of H100 peak, gives roughly 220 node-hours, and attention over long contexts adds more, so 200 to 500. TabPFN-3.5, TabFM, Kumo Tabular and LimiX-2 report no compute. For comparison, these are tiny budgets next to large language models. The inference column comes from one source, NVIDIA's chart of median inference time per thousand rows on a single RTX 6000 Pro, so those numbers are comparable with each other but self-reported by NVIDIA. LimiX-2 and TabFM+ take seven to nine seconds per thousand rows; Kumo Tabular Small, TabICLv2 and TabPFN-3.5-Fast take a tenth of a second or so. Models not in that chart are left blank because their papers time different things; TabPFN v2's paper, for example, reports 2.8 seconds for a whole fit-and-predict run per dataset. The right columns give the documented inference limits and the largest tables seen in pretraining; most models were trained on far smaller tables than they are used on, which is why long-context tricks matter.
